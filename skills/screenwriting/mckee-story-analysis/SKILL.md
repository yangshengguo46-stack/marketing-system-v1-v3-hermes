# McKee Story Analysis

## When to Use
- Evaluating an existing script's dramatic structure
- Planning a new script before writing
- Diagnosing why a story feels flat or unengaging
- Strengthening the central conflict and stakes

## Core Framework: McKee's Story Grammar

### The Five Core Elements

1. **Hook** — The opening situation that creates curiosity. What makes the audience lean in?
2. **Gap** — The distance between expectation and reality. The protagonist expects one thing, gets another. This gap creates tension.
3. **Crisis** — The moment of maximum pressure. The protagonist must make an irreversible choice under impossible conditions.
4. **Intervention** — The climactic action. The protagonist crosses the gap, transformed by the journey.
5. **Resolution** — The new equilibrium. The world has changed; the protagonist has changed.

### Analysis Process

#### Phase 1: Foundation (Skills 01-05)

1. **Genre Identification**
   - Identify the primary genre (Love Story, Horror, Comedy, Thriller, etc.)
   - Determine the genre's core promise to the audience
   - Note any genre hybridization

2. **Premise & Controlling Idea**
   - Premise: What is the story about in one sentence?
   - Controlling Idea: What value + cause drives the story?
   - Example: "Justice triumphs (value) because the protagonist perseveres despite corruption (cause)"

3. **Inciting Incident**
   - What event disrupts the protagonist's world?
   - Does it create a clear gap between desire and reality?

4. **Progressive Complications**
   - List 3-5 escalating obstacles
   - Each must raise stakes, not just add difficulty
   - Verify each complication creates a new gap

5. **Central Conflict**
   - Protagonist's desire vs. opposing force
   - Inner conflict (self vs. self)
   - Personal conflict (self vs. intimates)
   - Extra-personal conflict (self vs. society/nature/institutions)

#### Phase 2: Character (Skills 06-13)

6. **Character Arc**
   - Beginning state (who they are)
   - Crisis choice (who they must become)
   - End state (who they've become)
   - Is the change irreversible?

7. **Character Dimensions**
   - First dimension: What they show the world (surface)
   - Second dimension: What they know about themselves (contradictions)
   - Third dimension: What the audience discovers (subtext)

8. **Desire vs. Need**
   - Conscious desire (what they say they want)
   - Unconscious need (what they actually need)
   - The gap between these drives the arc

9. **Character Relationships**
   - Map all relationships (ally, antagonist, mentor, shapeshifter)
   - Each relationship should create a different kind of pressure
   - No relationship should be static

10. **Dialogue Voice**
    - Does each character sound distinct?
    - Is subtext present (saying one thing, meaning another)?
    - Is exposition delivered through conflict, not lectures?

11. **Character Empathy**
    - What makes the audience care about this character?
    - Empathy through: recognition, fascination, vicarious desire, or fear

12. **Antagonist Analysis**
    - Is the antagonist as complex as the protagonist?
    - Does the antagonist believe they are the hero of their own story?
    - Is the antagonist's power proportional to the protagonist's need?

13. **Supporting Cast**
    - Does each supporting character serve a narrative function?
    - Are any characters redundant (can be merged)?
    - Do supporting characters reveal different facets of the protagonist?

#### Phase 3: Structure & Pacing (Skills 14-17)

14. **Three-Act Structure Check**
    - Act 1 (25%): Setup, inciting incident, first turning point
    - Act 2 (50%): Progressive complications, midpoint reversal, crisis
    - Act 3 (25%): Climax, resolution
    - Are the act breaks clear and motivated?

15. **Beat Sheet**
    - Map every scene as a beat (action → reaction)
    - Each beat must either advance the plot or reveal character
    - Cut any beat that does neither

16. **Pacing Analysis**
    - Where does the story drag? (Usually Act 2 middle)
    - Are tension levels escalating, not flat-lining?
    - Is there rhythm between action and reflection?

17. **Climax Evaluation**
    - Does the climax deliver on the controlling idea?
    - Is the protagonist's choice truly irreversible?
    - Does the climax feel earned, not arbitrary?

#### Phase 4: Dialogue & Polish (Skills 18-24)

18. **Subtext Audit**
    - Flag any dialogue that says exactly what it means
    - Rewrite to create layers of meaning
    - The audience should understand more than the characters say

19. **Conflict in Every Scene**
    - Every scene should have at least one conflict
    - If a scene has no conflict, cut it or add one
    - Conflict can be subtle (silence, avoidance, micro-expressions)

20. **Exposition Check**
    - Flag any "as you know, Bob" dialogue
    - Convert exposition to conflict (characters argue about the facts)
    - Show, don't tell — let actions reveal backstory

21. **Emotional Rhythm**
    - Track the emotional journey beat by beat
    - Ensure variety: tension, release, tension, release
    - The lowest emotional point should precede the climax

22. **Thematic Resonance**
    - Does the theme emerge through action, not speech?
    - Is the theme tested from multiple angles?
    - Does the ending pay off the theme?

23. **Title & Logline**
    - Logline: [Protagonist] + [Inciting Incident] + [Goal] + [Central Conflict] + [Stakes]
    - Title: Does it evoke the theme without being on-the-nose?

24. **Director's Notes**
    - Visual motifs that reinforce theme
    - Key emotional beats for actors
    - Pacing notes for editor
    - Sound design suggestions

## Output Format

After analysis, produce:
1. **Story Diagnosis** — strengths and weaknesses
2. **Beat Board** — visual sequence of key moments
3. **Director's Notes** — production guidance
4. **Revision Checklist** — prioritized action items
